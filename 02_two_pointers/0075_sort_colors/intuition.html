Three pointers, four parts

While you walk through the array once, three pointers split it into four parts: finished 0s on the left, finished 1s after them, a part you have not looked at yet, and finished 2s on the right. Every step looks at one value and makes the unseen part one cell smaller.

  • low is the next free place for a 0. Everything to its left is finished 0s. It starts at the first position.
  • i is the cell you are looking at right now.
  • high is the next free place for a 2. Everything to its right is finished 2s. It starts at the last position, because no 2s are placed yet.
finished 0s (left of low) finished 1s (low up to i) not looked at yet (i to high) finished 2s (right of high)

    Why i moves after one swap but not after the other

    Look at what lands at position i after each swap.

    • Swap with low (you found a 0). The value that comes back to i was at low. Everything from low up to i is the 1s part, so that value is a 1. You already know it, and it belongs where it now is. So i moves on.
    • Swap with high (you found a 2). The value that comes back to i was at high, inside the part you have not looked at. It could be 0, 1 or 2. So i stays and looks at it on the next step.

    Pick "0s after 1s" above and step to the moment a 0 is swapped with a 1. Then pick "All 2s" and watch i stay at position 0 while high moves left three times.

    Is this really a medium?

    The counting version you wrote is easy. The one-pass version is rated medium for one reason: you have to keep four parts correct after every single swap, and the rule for i is different after each kind of swap. The code is about ten lines. Getting those ten lines right without the picture above is the hard part, and that is what interviewers are checking.

    This idea has a name: the Dutch national flag problem, from Edsger Dijkstra. The same three-part split appears again in quicksort partitioning with many equal values.
