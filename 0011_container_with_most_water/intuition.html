Why the shorter wall moves

The scan starts with one pointer at each end. Every step measures one container and throws one wall away for good. Step through it and check each step: the wall it throws away never had a bigger container left in it.

area(i, j) = (j − i) × min(h[i], h[j])

The argument, without the pictures

  1. Say h[L] ≤ h[R]. The container (L, R) holds (R − L) × h[L], and we've just measured it.
  2. Any other container that still uses bar L pairs it with some k between them. It is narrower (k − L < R − L) and its water can't rise above h[L].
  3. So it holds no more than (L, R). Bar L has nothing better left to offer, and dropping it can't lose the answer.

The grid shows why nothing slips through. The pairs still in play always form a triangle with (L, R) in its top-right corner. Dropping L deletes that triangle's top row; dropping R deletes its right column. After n − 1 steps the triangle is empty, and every pair was either measured or ruled out by a measured pair that's at least as big. The largest measured area is therefore the largest of all.

The taller wall can't be dropped the same way. Its partners can be taller than the short wall, so a narrower container could still hold more. Try Example 1: dropping bar 7 (height 6) first would skip (1, 7) = 36.

When both walls are the same height, the argument works for either one, so it doesn't matter which pointer moves.
